An AI-built website uses a generative tool to help create its structure, copy,
visual direction or code. For a business owner, it can be a practical way to
explore an idea and produce an initial version. The decision to build it yourself
or hire a developer depends on what the product must do and who can take
responsibility for it after launch.

Suppose you ask Codex or Claude Code to create a service page. It looks
convincing: a headline, cards, a form and suitable colours. The next question is
whether it is ready to use. The same applies to a small web app: generating an
order dashboard and relying on it for real orders are different moments in a
product's life.

You can assess that boundary without learning to program.

## When building it yourself makes sense

A simple page with a clear offer, prepared content and a straightforward contact
path can be a reasonable DIY project. This works especially well when you are
willing to manage the content, publishing and small corrections yourself.

There are several ways to approach it:

- **A website builder such as Wix, Squarespace or Tilda.** You work within the
  platform's editing and publishing model. Check the functions and terms of
  the specific plan before choosing it.
- **Your own implementation with Codex or Claude Code.** You have more control
  over the implementation and take decisions about its structure, hosting and
  checks. For example,
  [Claude Code's documentation](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/overview)
  describes working with project code, files and commands.
- **Commissioned development.** You delegate an agreed outcome, including the
  intended scope of discovery, implementation, verification, launch and handover.
  Confirm those responsibilities in the proposal.

A DIY experiment can also help you explore an application using fictional data:
is the calculation useful, does the interface make sense, is the idea worth
developing? You have more room to experiment when restarting has little cost.

Commissioned work may also use a website builder or AI. The way code is produced
is one part of the process. The responsibility being transferred needs its own
discussion.

## What to check behind the attractive screen

### Can visitors understand your offer?

A page can contain all the familiar sections while leaving people unsure who
the service is for, what they receive and why they should contact you. The next
iteration then needs to improve the structure and content.

Review it from a prospective customer's perspective. What information do they
need before making an enquiry? The price or its basis, examples, limitations,
the next step? Check that the copy describes your actual business. Generated
testimonials, figures and promises should be replaced with verifiable material
or removed.

### Does the design follow consistent rules?

Good design helps people find their way. Important actions are visible, text is
comfortable to read and familiar elements behave consistently. Colour,
typography and imagery support a coherent brand direction.

Check the whole journey: how it works on a phone, what happens with a longer
heading, whether the form is comfortable to complete and whether errors are
understandable. [W3C's WCAG guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/quickref/)
provides concrete criteria for contrast, keyboard navigation and field labels.

Consistency comes from shared styles and components used throughout the project.
That system becomes valuable when you add a service, another page or a new
application state. During a demonstration, ask how such a change would fit the
existing interface.

### Does each action reach a real outcome?

An enquiry button should lead to an enquiry you actually receive. A success
message on the page does not prove delivery. Submit a test enquiry and check it
at the receiving end.

An application requires more questions. Imagine a small order tool: an employee
creates an order, updates its status and the owner reviews the result. Does the
order survive a page refresh? Who can change it? What happens after a double
click? How does the tool report a failed save?

Quality is visible in these behaviours. A useful demonstration includes the
normal journey and interruptions: invalid input, an empty list and an action
without the necessary access. Choose checks that match your product's functions.

### Can it handle real business data?

Once an application stores contact details, documents or business records, access,
storage, backups and recovery need attention. A hidden button does not explain
how the system controls access to the underlying data.

[OWASP's Application Security Verification Standard](https://owasp.org/projects/asvs)
sets out requirements for verifying web application security. For an owner, the
practical implication is that data protection and access should have specific
checks and someone responsible for them. Ask what “secure” means for your
particular product.

The depth depends on the purpose. A page containing public information and an
application where employees can see different orders need different controls.

### Can someone continue the work?

A maintainable product has source code, a history of changes and understandable
rules for how it is organised. Someone can find where a text is edited, a price
is calculated or an enquiry is delivered. Important decisions are recorded, and
key journeys can be checked again.

For the owner, this supports updates and handover to another developer. Checks
help reveal when a new feature breaks an existing behaviour. They are useful
only when they actually detect a fault in the scenario being checked.

Ask to see the source, setup instructions, external-service list and update
process. If the application stores data, ask how recovery from a backup works.
Those materials should describe the version being delivered.

## What a systematic process adds to AI-assisted work

A controlled process starts by describing the expected behaviour: who uses the
product, what they do, what result they receive and which boundaries apply.
Implementation is then selected and checked against that intent.

AI can assist different parts of this work. Requirements, design and code rules,
checks and change history stay in the project, giving the next update something
to build on. Decisions affecting data, permissions and the primary journey need
deliberate review by the person responsible for delivery.

As a client, you should be able to inspect the outputs of that process: agreed
scope, a behaviour demonstration, problems found and corrected, and a handover
plan. A claim that a project follows rules becomes meaningful when you can see
which properties those rules help preserve.

This applies to both generated and manually written code. Human involvement
does not automatically establish quality. Verification provides evidence.

## When professional help adds value

Help is particularly useful when you need to connect business systems, introduce
users and roles, keep important records or change the product regularly. A lack
of time for checks and maintenance is also a valid reason to delegate, even if
you can build the first version yourself.

Commission a clear scope of help. Sometimes a prototype review and a prioritised
problem list are enough. Sometimes one journey needs completing and preparing
for launch. In other cases, replacing a part of the implementation makes more
sense. Reviewing the source and behaviour helps establish the right approach.

Your prototype remains useful evidence of the idea, desired workflow and visual
choices. Its appearance alone cannot establish the effort needed to finish it.
An offer to “just tidy it up” needs that assessment first.

## Make the choice around your actual task

Start with the outcome: what must a visitor or employee successfully do? Then
consider the consequence of an error and your capacity to look after the product
once it is live.

A simple page you can update may work well in a website builder. An AI experiment
can clarify an application idea. For a tool used in daily operations, define who
owns checks, updates and recovery before people rely on it.
